Total cost: the 3–7% rule

Add up every fee below and most estates land at 3–7% of the estate's total value. On a $500,000 estate, that's roughly $15,000–$35,000 in combined costs. Two patterns matter:

  • Small estates pay a higher percentage. Fixed costs (filing fees, minimum attorney charges) don't shrink with the estate.
  • Very large estates pay a lower percentage — except in states with statutory percentage fee schedules (California, Florida), where attorney fees scale with the estate.

Attorney fees: the biggest line item

Attorney fees are usually the largest single probate cost. How they're charged depends on the state:

Fee structureTypical rangeWhere it's common
Hourly$250–$500/hourMost states; total $3,000–$7,000+ for routine estates
Flat fee$3,000–$7,000+Simple, uncontested estates
Statutory percentageSet by law (e.g. 4% of first $100K in CA)California, Florida and a few others — much higher on large estates

The statutory-percentage states deserve attention: California Probate Code §10810 sets attorney compensation at 4% of the first $100,000 of estate value, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, and 1% of the next $9 million — so on a $1,000,000 California estate, the statutory schedule alone produces a five-figure fee before any "extraordinary" work is billed. Florida has a similar statutory schedule. In hourly/flat-fee states, shopping around and getting a written fee agreement up front is the single best cost control.

Court costs and filing fees

Court fees are set by each state's court system and are the most predictable cost: typically $200–$1,200 to open probate, plus smaller fees for certified copies, notices, and miscellaneous filings. These are non-negotiable but small relative to the total.

Executor compensation

Executors (personal representatives) are entitled to compensation in most states — either set by statute as a percentage, or a "reasonable" amount approved by the court. Typical: 2–4% of the estate value, though many family-member executors waive the fee. Corporate or professional executors charge at the top of the range.

Where families overpay (and how to reduce costs)

  1. No written fee agreement. Always get the attorney's fee structure in writing before work starts — hourly with an estimate, or flat.
  2. Disorganized executors. Every hour the attorney spends hunting down statements and deeds is billed. An organized file of accounts, titles, and debts cuts billable hours substantially.
  3. Contests and disputes. A will contest can double or triple total costs. Mediation is cheaper than litigation when disagreements arise.
  4. Skipping small-estate procedures. If the estate qualifies for simplified probate, full formal probate wastes thousands. Check your state's threshold first.
  5. No advance planning. The cheapest probate is the one that never happens — living trusts and beneficiary designations move assets outside probate entirely.

Testate vs. intestate costs

Dying without a will (intestate) doesn't just slow probate down — it makes it more expensive. Intestate estates more often need court hearings to establish heirs, require administrator bonds, and generate the family disputes that drive attorney hours up. As a rough planning matter, expect an intestate probate to cost 20–40% more than a comparable testate one — another reason estate planning pays for itself.

FAQ

What percentage does probate take from an estate?

Total probate costs typically run 3–7% of the estate's value. Small estates pay a higher percentage; very large estates tend to pay a lower one.

Who pays probate attorney fees?

The estate pays. Attorney fees, court costs, and executor compensation come out of estate assets before heirs receive anything — heirs don't pay out of pocket.

How can probate costs be reduced?

Uncontested estates with organized executors cost far less. Advance planning — living trusts, beneficiary designations — avoids probate costs entirely on those assets.

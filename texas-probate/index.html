Texas is one of the least expensive states to probate. Its signature feature — independent administration — lets executors work largely free of court supervision, which is why a simple Texas probate can cost under $5,000 where the same estate in California or Florida would cost five figures.

Choosing wrong wastes money: paying for a full independent administration when a small estate affidavit would do, or — worse — skipping probate for years and discovering the 4-year deadline has narrowed your options (see the 4-year deadline below).

Independent administration: the express lane

Independent administration — governed by Texas Estates Code Chapters 401–405 — is what makes Texas probate fast and cheap. After the court appoints the executor and the inventory, appraisement, and list of claims is filed, the executor collects assets, pays debts, and distributes property without asking a judge for permission for each action. The Estates Code describes the independent executor as free from "the expense and control of judicial supervision" except where the Code specifically provides otherwise.

  • Who can use it: an executor named as "independent" in the will — or, without a will, an administrator appointed independently when all heirs agree.
  • Bond: most independent executors do not post one.
  • Typical timeline: 6–12 months from filing to final distribution for uncontested estates; the initial hearing often occurs 6–8 weeks after the application is filed.
  • Typical cost: practitioners commonly report total attorney fees under $5,000 for straightforward independent administrations, versus 40–70% higher for dependent administration of a comparable estate.

This is why Texas estate planners emphasize the "magic language" in wills appointing an independent executor: it converts the process from supervised administration to a near-paperwork proceeding.

Muniment of title: probating only to clear title

Sometimes the only reason to touch probate court is to prove who owns the house. When that's the case, Texas offers probate of the will as a muniment of title (Texas Estates Code Chapter 257, §257.001): the court admits the will to establish title to the decedent's property, and appoints no executor or administrator at all. The proceeding typically concludes after a single hearing.

  • Requirements: a valid will (generally offered within four years of death — see §256.003 below); the estate must owe no unpaid debts other than debts secured by liens on real estate (a mortgage is fine; an outstanding credit card balance, unpaid medical bills, or a Medicaid estate recovery claim disqualifies the estate); and the court must find there is no necessity for administration.
  • Court filing fees: the state-mandated consolidated fee is $223 local + $137 state = $360 for a new probate case per the Texas Judicial Branch, with county-specific add-ons bringing the typical total to $300–$450, plus certified copies and any citation fees.
  • Attorney fees: uncontested muniments are commonly handled on a flat fee since the matter is routine — getting quotes from two or three probate attorneys in the county is the standard advice.

Small estate affidavit: under $75,000

When someone dies without a will and the estate is small, Texas Estates Code Chapter 205 lets heirs skip formal probate with a small estate affidavit. All six conditions in §205.001 must be true:

  1. The decedent died intestate (no valid will of any kind).
  2. At least 30 days have passed since the date of death.
  3. No petition to appoint a personal representative is pending or has been granted.
  4. The estate's non-exempt assets — excluding the homestead and exempt personal property — are worth $75,000 or less on the date the affidavit is filed.
  5. Those non-exempt assets exceed the estate's known debts (again excluding homestead-secured debts).
  6. The affidavit (sworn by two disinterested witnesses and every distributee with legal capacity) is filed with the court clerk and approved by the judge (§205.002–205.004).

Two catches families miss: verify the $75,000 limit against the current §205.001 text before relying on it; and if the estate owns any real property beyond the homestead (a rental, a vacant lot), the affidavit cannot clear title to it. The homestead is the only real property a small estate affidavit can transfer, and only when it's the estate's sole real property (§205.006).

Dependent administration: full court supervision

Dependent administration is the fallback when independence isn't available: the personal representative needs court permission for most significant actions — property sales, debt payments, final distribution. It's required when heirs won't agree to independent administration, when creditor claims need judicial sorting, or when the court finds supervision necessary.

  • Cost: practitioner analyses put dependent administration at roughly 40–70% more expensive than independent administration of a comparable estate, driven by hearings, motions, and repeated attorney work.
  • Timeline: often a year or more, versus 6–12 months for independent administration.
  • Bond: the court typically requires one, adding the annual premium to the bill.

Anything that converts an estate from independent to dependent — a will contest, an heirship fight, creditor litigation — converts the cost profile with it.

Who inherits without a will in Texas

Texas is a community property state, and intestate shares depend on whether property is community or separate. The rules below are from Texas Estates Code §§201.002–201.003 (deaths on or after September 1, 1993):

SituationCommunity propertySeparate property
Surviving spouse, all children also the spouse'sAll to the surviving spouse (§201.003(b)(2))Spouse takes 1/3 of personal property; children take 2/3; spouse gets a life estate in 1/3 of the land, remainder to children (§201.002(b))
Surviving spouse, decedent has a child from another relationshipSpouse keeps own 1/2; decedent's 1/2 to the children (§201.003(c))Same as above (§201.002(b))
Surviving spouse, no childrenSpouse's 1/2 + decedent's 1/2 generally to spouse if no surviving parents/siblings — see statuteSpouse takes all personal property + 1/2 the land outright; other 1/2 of land goes to parents/siblings; if none, spouse takes all (§201.002(c)–(d))

Intestate administration is consistently more expensive than testate: heirship must be established in court (Chapter 202, with an attorney ad litem), bonds are more likely, and disputes are more common.

The 4-year filing deadline

Texas Estates Code §256.003: a will may not be admitted to probate after the fourth anniversary of the testator's death unless the applicant proves they were "not in default" — that the delay wasn't due to a lack of reasonable diligence. Practical consequences:

  • Within four years: standard probate (independent administration, muniment of title, or letters testamentary).
  • After four years, with a valid excuse (e.g., nobody knew the will existed): the will can typically still be admitted — but only as a muniment of title. Under §256.003(b)/§301.002, letters testamentary cannot issue after four years. No executor gets appointed.
  • After four years, in default: the court denies probate entirely, and the estate passes under intestacy law — requiring a formal heirship proceeding, an attorney ad litem, and a distribution the decedent may not have wanted.

The deadline risk is highest for surviving spouses who delay because community property is passing smoothly — while separate property named in the will sits in limbo. When in doubt, file before the window closes.

FAQ

How much does probate cost in Texas?

A straightforward independent administration in Texas often costs under $5,000 in attorney fees plus about $300–$450 in court filing fees. Muniment of title proceedings cost less. Dependent administrations, heirship proceedings, and contested cases cost substantially more.

How long does probate take in Texas?

An uncontested independent administration typically takes 6–12 months from filing to final distribution, with a court hearing often 6–8 weeks after filing. Contested or dependent administrations can take a year or more.

What is the deadline to probate a will in Texas?

Texas Estates Code §256.003 generally requires a will to be admitted to probate within four years of the testator's death. After four years, a will can only be admitted if the applicant proves they were not in default in failing to present it sooner — and then only as a muniment of title, not for administration.

What is the Texas small estate affidavit limit?

$75,000. Under Texas Estates Code §205.001, heirs of an intestate decedent can use a small estate affidavit — instead of formal probate — when non-exempt estate assets (excluding the homestead and exempt property) are worth $75,000 or less, at least 30 days have passed since death, and the estate owes no debts beyond those secured by the homestead.

How Texas compares nationally

No statutory attorney-fee percentage (unlike California's §10810 schedule), a $75,000 small-estate threshold, and independent administration make Texas one of the cheapest states to probate. See the national cost breakdown and the probate timeline.

Why probate varies so much by state

Probate is state law. Three things differ everywhere you look:

  1. Attorney fee rules. Most states let attorneys charge a "reasonable fee" (often hourly or flat). California and Florida set fees by statute as a percentage of the estate — on a $1M estate that schedule alone runs deep into five figures.
  2. Court costs and creditor periods. Filing fees range from a couple hundred dollars to over a thousand, and the mandatory creditor-claim window runs 3–6 months depending on the state — that window alone sets a floor under the timeline.
  3. Small-estate shortcuts. Every state has a simplified process under a dollar threshold, but the thresholds range from $50,000 to $184,500+ — which decides whether many families probate at all.

Common questions

Do all estates have to go through probate?

No. Assets with named beneficiaries (life insurance, retirement accounts, payable-on-death accounts), jointly owned property with survivorship rights, and assets in a living trust bypass probate. Small estates often qualify for simplified procedures.

Can you avoid probate entirely?

Often, yes — with advance planning: a revocable living trust, beneficiary designations, and joint ownership are the standard tools. But each has trade-offs, and "avoid probate at all costs" is bad advice for some families.

Who pays for probate?

The estate pays — attorney fees, court costs, and executor compensation come out of estate assets before anything is distributed to heirs. Heirs don't pay out of pocket.

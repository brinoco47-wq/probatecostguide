California is one of two states (with Florida) that price probate attorneys by statutory percentage schedule rather than by hours. Three California features shape every estimate: the fee is computed on gross value (mortgages don't count), the schedule applies twice (attorney + representative), and the inflation-indexed small-estate thresholds — which depend on the date of death — keep many modest estates out of court entirely.

The gross-value rule: your mortgage doesn't lower the bill

The single most misunderstood fact about California probate: statutory fees are calculated on the estate's gross value — "without reference to encumbrances or other obligations on estate property." The mortgage is invisible to the math.

The example every California practitioner uses: a house appraised at $800,000 with $500,000 still owed produces the same attorney fee as a paid-off $800,000 house, even though the family's real equity is only $300,000. In high-value housing markets, this is why an estate that feels modest can produce a decidedly immodest probate bill — and why the "4–7% of estate value" national rule of thumb breaks down for debt-heavy California estates.

The statutory attorney fee schedule (§10810)

California Probate Code §10810 fixes the attorney's compensation for ordinary services (filing the petition, giving creditor notice, inventory, paying debts, distribution) on a graduated scale. The tiers stack:

Portion of estate valueRateMaximum on that portion

Next $100,0003%$3,000

Next $9,000,0001%$90,000

The schedule math, re-checked against the statute: a $300,000 estate gives 4% × $100,000 + 3% × $100,000 + 2% × $100,000 = $9,000; a $500,000 estate gives $4,000 + $3,000 + (2% × $300,000) = $13,000; a $750,000 estate gives $4,000 + $3,000 + (2% × $550,000) = $18,000; a $1 million estate gives $4,000 + $3,000 + (2% × $800,000) = $23,000; a $2 million estate gives $23,000 + (1% × $1,000,000) = $33,000. Above the ordinary-services line, the schedule is a floor, not a ceiling — see extraordinary fees below.

The personal representative gets the same fee (§10800)

Probate Code §10800 entitles the personal representative (executor or administrator) to compensation on the identical schedule — so in practice the statutory fee is charged twice, once to the attorney and once to the representative. On a $500,000 estate that's $13,000 + $13,000 = $26,000; on $1 million, $46,000; on $2 million, $66,000 — before a single court cost.

Two qualifications that materially change the real price: a family member serving as representative routinely waives the fee (many waive for tax reasons, since inherited distributions aren't income but fees are), cutting the statutory total nearly in half — and the attorney's fee is negotiable in theory, though in practice attorneys rarely discount the schedule for ordinary services. Ask about the waiver before assuming the doubled figure is the real price.

Extraordinary fees: when the schedule isn't the whole bill

§10810 covers ordinary services only. For unusual work, the attorney (and the representative, under §10801) can petition the court for extraordinary fees under Probate Code §10811 — billed on top of the schedule, at the attorney's hourly rate, and subject to court approval. Qualifying work includes:

  • Selling probate real estate and handling the escrow
  • Will contests and other litigation, defending the accounting
  • Tax work — preparing the decedent's final return, fiduciary returns, or the federal estate tax return (Form 706)
  • Operating the decedent's business during administration
  • Multi-state assets and ancillary proceedings

Practitioners' rule of thumb: a contested probate routinely doubles or triples the total cost of a routine one. The schedule number is the starting bid, not the ceiling.

Skipping probate: the small-estate shortcuts

California doesn't have one probate cutoff — it has a family of shortcuts, each with its own dollar limit. The headline limits are indexed for inflation under Probate Code §890, so the date of death decides which number applies:

Date of deathPersonal-property affidavit limit (§13100)
Before April 1, 2022$166,250
April 1, 2022 – March 31, 2025$184,500
On or after April 1, 2025$208,850

The three transfer paths, all measured on gross value:

  1. Small estate affidavit (§13100): after a 40-day wait, heirs collect bank accounts, securities, vehicles and other personal property worth up to $208,850 with a sworn affidavit — no court filing, no court fee. Joint-tenancy, trust and beneficiary-designated property are excluded from the count. Whoever collects becomes personally liable for the decedent's unsecured debts up to the value received (§§13109–13110).
  2. Real property of small value (§13200): California real estate worth $69,625 or less can transfer by a court petition (petition to determine succession) — faster and cheaper than full administration.
  3. Primary residence petition (§13151, AB 2016): the decedent's primary residence worth up to $750,000 can transfer by court petition — a newer shortcut (deaths on/after April 1, 2025) that cuts the timeline from 12–18 months to roughly 2–6 months for the family home. Investment and second homes don't qualify.

Two more tools worth knowing: the Spousal Property Petition (§13650) transfers community, quasi-community, and separate property passing to a surviving spouse or domestic partner with no dollar limit — California's most powerful shortcut for married couples. And the Heggstad petition (§850) cures the common "oops" where an asset was meant to be in the living trust (say, listed on Schedule A) but was never retitled — the court confirms it as trust property without opening a full probate estate.

Community property and the spousal petition

California is a community property state: property acquired during the marriage generally belongs to both spouses equally. That changes the probate math in two ways:

  • Half the marital estate starts with the surviving spouse. The surviving spouse's community share isn't an inheritance — it's already theirs — so far less of a married couple's wealth is exposed to probate than in common-law states.
  • The Spousal Property Petition (§13650) is the streamlined route for confirming community property, quasi-community property, and any separate property passing to the spouse. No dollar cap, no four-month creditor clock — the standard answer for a surviving spouse is a petition, not a probate.

Separate property — owned before the marriage, inherited, or received as a gift — still passes by will or intestate succession, and still needs probate (or a shortcut) when it exceeds the small-estate limits.

IAEA: full vs. limited authority

The Independent Administration of Estates Act lets the personal representative manage most estate affairs without asking the court each time. It is requested on the initial Petition for Probate (Judicial Council form DE-111) and is granted unless an interested person objects and shows good cause. Two flavors:

AuthorityWhat it meansWhy it matters
Full authoritySell property, pay debts, and take most actions after a Notice of Proposed Action + 15-day wait — no hearingHouse sales close without court confirmation; shaves months off the timeline
Limited authoritySame powers except selling or exchanging real property still needs a court confirmation hearing (with possible courtroom overbidding)Slower, more hearings, higher cost

Practitioners' consensus: always request full authority. It's the difference between selling the house with a notice and selling it with a hearing — the single biggest lever on the timeline.

Creditor claims: 4 months, then 60 days

California gives creditors the longest standard claim window of any major state, and it sets the floor under the whole timeline:

  • 4 months from the date letters are first issued to a general personal representative — the headline deadline (Prob. Code §9100(a)).
  • 60 days after the representative mails or personally delivers notice of administration to a known creditor — if later than the four-month mark, this controls (§9100(b)).
  • Known or reasonably ascertainable creditors must be given actual notice (§9050) — publication alone isn't enough, and the representative has a duty to make a reasonably diligent effort to find them (§9053). Miss one, and a late claim can reopen the estate's books (§9103).
  • One-year outer limit: Code of Civil Procedure §366.2 bars most claims against a decedent filed more than a year after death, whether or not probate was opened.

The practical consequence: even a cooperative estate can't safely close before the four-month window runs, and the Medi-Cal recovery program (DHCS) must be notified within 90 days of death when it may hold a claim — a deadline that trips up otherwise-clean cases.

No California estate tax

California has no estate tax, no inheritance tax, and no gift tax. Voters repealed the state inheritance tax with Proposition 6 in 1982; the remaining "pick-up" tax piggybacked on a federal credit that Congress phased out, and no California estate tax return has been required for deaths on or after January 1, 2005. Estates are subject only to the federal estate tax above the federal exemption. The expensive part of a California death is the probate bill, not the tax bill.

FAQ

How much does probate cost in California?

California sets attorney fees by statute (§10810): 4% of the first $100,000, 3% of the next $100,000, 2% of the next $800,000, then 1%. On a $500,000 estate the schedule gives $13,000 in attorney fees, and the personal representative is entitled to an identical $13,000 under §10800 — $26,000 in statutory fees before court costs. Fees are computed on gross value, so a mortgage does not reduce them.

How long does probate take in California?

Most California probates take 12 to 18 months from filing to final distribution. The four-month creditor claim period (§9100) plus court hearing schedules set a practical floor of about seven months; simple estates may close in 9–12 months, while contested estates run 2–3 years. Full authority under the Independent Administration of Estates Act speeds property sales by avoiding confirmation hearings.

What is the small estate limit in California?

For deaths on or after April 1, 2025, heirs can collect up to $208,850 in personal property with a sworn affidavit under §13100 (no court filing, 40-day wait). Real property worth $69,625 or less can transfer by court petition under §13200, and a primary residence worth up to $750,000 can transfer by petition under §13151. A surviving spouse can use the Spousal Property Petition (§13650) with no dollar limit.

Does California have an estate tax?

No. California voters repealed the state inheritance tax with Proposition 6 in 1982, and the remaining pick-up estate tax disappeared for deaths on or after January 1, 2005. California has no estate tax, no inheritance tax, and no gift tax — only the federal estate tax applies, above the federal exemption.

Do I need a lawyer for probate in California?

Not legally — California, unlike Florida, does not require the personal representative to hire counsel. But probate here is procedure-heavy, and the attorney's statutory fee under §10810 applies whether or not you retain one. Most families hire counsel; the cost of a missed deadline usually exceeds the fee.

How California compares nationally

California and Florida are the only states that price probate attorneys by statutory percentage — and California's gross-value rule plus its 12–18 month timeline make it the most expensive probate in the country for mid-size estates. See the national cost breakdown and the probate timeline.

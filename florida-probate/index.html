Florida is one of two states (with California) that price probate attorneys by statutory percentage schedule rather than by hours. On small estates the schedule is modest — $3,000 at the top of the fixed-fee tiers — but on a seven-figure estate it produces a five-figure fee by law. Two uniquely Florida features change the math: summary administration lets small or old estates skip most of the process, and homestead protection can take the family home out of the equation entirely.

One more Florida quirk: the personal representative in formal administration generally must hire a Florida attorney — a representative may act without counsel only when they are the sole interested person (Florida Probate Rule 5.030(a)). Budget for counsel; self-representation is not an option for most estates.

The statutory attorney fee schedule

Florida Statute §733.6171 gives a "presumed reasonable" fee schedule for ordinary services in formal administration, computed on the estate's compensable value (inventory value plus estate income). The tiers stack:

Estate valuePresumed reasonable fee
$40,000 or less$1,500
More than $40,000 up to $70,000An additional $750 (total $2,250)
More than $70,000 up to $100,000An additional $750 (total $3,000)
$100,000 – $1 million3% of the amount over $100,000
$1 million – $3 million2.5% of the amount over $1 million
$3 million – $5 million2% of the amount over $3 million
$5 million – $10 million1.5% of the amount over $5 million
Above $10 million1% of the excess

The schedule math, re-checked against the statute text: a $400,000 estate gives $1,500 + $750 + $750 + (3% × $300,000) = $12,000; a $500,000 estate gives $3,000 + (3% × $400,000) = $15,000; a $1 million estate gives $3,000 + (3% × $900,000) = $30,000. Two caveats: "presumed reasonable" is a benchmark, not a mandate — attorneys can negotiate hourly or flat fees with the required written disclosures — and extraordinary services (will contests, real estate sales, tax work) are billed on top.

Personal representative compensation

The personal representative is separately entitled to a presumed-reasonable commission under Fla. Stat. §733.617, also percentage-based:

Estate valuePresumed reasonable commission
First $1 million3%
$1 million – $5 million2.5%
$5 million – $10 million2%
Above $10 million1.5%

On a $500,000 estate that's another $15,000 — which is why the schedule total looks alarming. In practice, a family member serving as representative routinely waives the commission, cutting the headline figure nearly in half. Ask about this before assuming the statutory total is the real price.

Summary administration: under $75,000

Summary administration (Chapter 735) is the Florida express lane. It's available when:

  1. The value of the estate subject to administration in Florida, less exempt property, is $75,000 or less — exempt property, notably homestead, doesn't count toward the limit (§735.201); or
  2. The decedent has been dead for more than two years, regardless of estate value — an escape hatch many families miss.

No personal representative is appointed; the court issues an order of summary administration distributing assets directly. Filing fees are lower ($340 for estates of $1,000+, $230 under $1,000 per §28.2401), attorney fees are typically flat and modest, and the process typically concludes in 1–3 months (practitioner planning range). If your estate qualifies, there is almost no reason to use formal administration.

Homestead: the Florida twist

Florida's homestead protection is the most generous in the country and it reshapes probate here:

  • Creditor shield: homestead is exempt from the claims of creditors (Fla. Const. art. X §4) — a creditor claim that would swallow other assets can't touch the home.
  • Restricted descent: when survived by a spouse or minor child, homestead cannot be devised away from them — it descends to the surviving spouse (a life estate, with the remainder to descendants) or outright to minor children, regardless of what the will says (§732.401).
  • Threshold math: because exempt property is excluded from the $75,000 summary-administration limit, a family home can keep an otherwise-over-threshold estate in the express lane.

Practitioners treat homestead status as the first question in any Florida estate — it determines what counts as estate value for fees, what creditors can reach, and who must inherit the house.

The surviving spouse's elective share

A spouse who is shortchanged by the will isn't stuck: Florida gives the surviving spouse a right to an elective share of 30% of the elective estate (§732.201). The "elective estate" is broader than the probate estate — it pulls in certain non-probate transfers (revocable trust assets, some joint property) so the share can't be gamed by keeping assets out of probate. Filing an election is formal and time-limited; a disinherited spouse should consult counsel promptly.

Who inherits without a will in Florida

Florida's intestate rules (Fla. Stat. §§732.102–732.103):

SituationShare
Surviving spouse, no descendantsSpouse takes everything (§732.102(1))
Surviving spouse + descendants who are all the spouse'sSpouse takes everything (§732.102(2))
Surviving spouse + decedent's descendants not the spouse'sSpouse takes 1/2; descendants take 1/2, per stirpes (§732.102(3))
No spouseDescendants take all, per stirpes (§732.103) — a deceased child's share passes to that child's children

Intestacy is consistently more expensive than testacy: heirship must be established, bonds are more likely, and contested shares trigger the extraordinary-fee tier above.

Creditor claims: 90 days vs 2 years

Two clocks run against creditors, and both protect the estate's timeline:

  • 90 days: once the notice to creditors is first published, creditors generally have 3 months to file claims (§733.702). The notice must be published weekly for two consecutive weeks in a county newspaper — publication typically costs $100–$250 (practitioner planning range).
  • 2 years: the statute of nonclaim bars claims filed more than two years after death, publication or not (§733.702(3)). This is why summary administration is available for 2-years-past deaths at any estate value — there is nothing left to wait for.

The 90-day window sets a practical floor: formal administration can't safely close before it runs, so even a simple estate needs roughly four to five months minimum.

FAQ

How much does probate cost in Florida?

Florida sets attorney fees by statute (§733.6171): $1,500 for estates up to $40,000, $2,250 up to $70,000, $3,000 up to $100,000, then 3% on the next $900,000. On a $500,000 estate the schedule gives $15,000 in attorney fees, plus about $399 in court filing fees and the personal representative's 3% commission — which a family-member representative often waives.

How long does probate take in Florida?

Formal administration in Florida typically takes 6 to 12 months for an uncontested estate. Creditors get 90 days to file claims after the notice to creditors is first published, which sets a practical floor under the timeline. Summary administration of small estates typically wraps up in 1 to 3 months.

What is summary administration in Florida?

Summary administration (§735.201) is a simplified procedure available when the estate subject to administration, less exempt property, is worth $75,000 or less — or when the decedent has been dead for more than two years, regardless of estate value. No personal representative is appointed and the process is substantially faster and cheaper than formal administration.

Does Florida require an attorney for probate?

In most cases, yes. A personal representative in formal administration must be represented by an admitted Florida attorney, except when the personal representative is the sole interested person (Florida Probate Rule 5.030(a)).

How Florida compares nationally

Florida and California are the only states that price probate attorneys by statutory percentage — and Florida's homestead protection and $75,000 summary-administration lane are among the most generous in the country. See the national cost breakdown and the probate timeline.

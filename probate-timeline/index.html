The overview: 9–18 months typical

Most probates land between 9 and 18 months from filing to final distribution. Three bands cover nearly every case:

Estate typeTypical timelineWhy
Simple, uncontested6–9 monthsStatutory creditor period is the floor; everything else moves fast
Average9–18 monthsProperty sale, tax filings, or slow paperwork add months
Complex or contested18 months – 3+ yearsWill contests, litigation, business interests, multi-state property

The 8 steps, in order

Step 1 — File the petition (weeks 1–4)

The executor (or interested person) files a petition with the probate court in the county where the deceased lived, along with the will and death certificate. The court issues letters testamentary (or letters of administration if there's no will), which give the executor legal authority to act. Typical: 2–4 weeks if the paperwork is complete; incomplete filings bounce back and restart the clock.

Step 2 — Notify heirs and creditors (weeks 3–8)

The executor notifies named heirs/beneficiaries and publishes a notice to creditors in a local newspaper, as most states require. Typical: 2–6 weeks to complete notifications.

Step 3 — Creditor claim period (months 2–8)

This is the step that sets the floor under every probate timeline. State law gives creditors a fixed window to file claims — commonly 3 to 6 months from first publication. The estate generally can't make final distributions until it closes. Nothing about executor efficiency shortens a statute.

Step 4 — Inventory and appraisal (months 2–6)

The executor inventories all probate assets and has them appraised at fair market value. Simple estates: a few weeks. Estates with real estate, businesses, or unusual assets: several months, especially if formal appraisals are required.

Step 5 — Pay debts and taxes (months 4–12)

Valid creditor claims get paid in the priority order state law sets. If estate tax returns are due (federal Form 706 for large estates, plus any state filings), this step waits on tax preparation — and the IRS routinely takes 6+ months to process estate tax returns.

Step 6 — Sell or distribute property (months 6–14)

Real estate is the usual bottleneck: listing, selling, and closing a house inside probate adds months, and court confirmation of the sale is required in some states. Personal property and accounts distribute faster.

Step 7 — Final accounting (months 10–16)

The executor prepares a final accounting of everything that came in and went out. Beneficiaries review and approve it (or the court does). Disputes here restart negotiations and add months.

Step 8 — Distribution and closing (months 12–18)

Remaining assets distribute to heirs, the court issues a final decree, and the estate closes. Receipts are filed. Done.

What causes delays

  • Will contests — the single biggest delay; litigation routinely adds 1–2 years.
  • Missing paperwork — no will, unclear beneficiary designations, untitled property.
  • Real estate in multiple states — requires ancillary probate in each state.
  • Family disagreement — even without a formal contest, disputes slow every signature.
  • Tax filings — estate tax returns and IRS processing times.
  • Slow executors — disorganized or unresponsive personal representatives stall everything.

Can probate be sped up?

Partly. An organized executor who files immediately, inventories early, lists property fast, and keeps beneficiaries informed can shave months off the discretionary steps. What can't be sped up: statutory creditor periods, court backlogs, and IRS processing. And the most effective "speed-up" happens before death — a funded living trust, updated beneficiary designations, and jointly titled property keep assets out of probate entirely.

FAQ

How long does probate take on average?

Most probates take 9–18 months. Simple, uncontested estates can close in about 6 months; contested or complex estates often run 2 years or more.

What is the longest part of probate?

Usually the creditor claim period (3–6 months by statute, during which little else can finalize) plus any waiting on real-estate sales or tax clearances.

Can probate be sped up?

An organized executor helps — filing promptly, inventorying early, and selling property quickly. But statutory waiting periods cannot be skipped.

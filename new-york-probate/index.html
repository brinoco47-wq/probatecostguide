New York is unusual: executor commissions are fixed by statute (like California's attorney schedule), while attorney fees are not scheduled at all. For mid-size estates the commission alone can be the single largest probate expense — and on seven-figure estates, New York's estate tax cliff can dwarf every other line item.

Three tracks: probate, administration, voluntary administration

New York runs three procedures through the Surrogate's Court of the county where the decedent lived:

TrackWhen it fitsRough cost
Probate (SCPA Articles 14–15)There is a will; the court admits it and issues Letters Testamentary to the named executorFull-cost: commissions + attorney + filing
Administration (SCPA Article 10)No will; the court issues Letters of Administration following the priority order in SCPA 1001, and property passes under the intestacy table (EPTL 4-1.1)Like probate; a court-ordered bond often adds cost
Voluntary administration (SCPA Article 13)Personal property worth $50,000 or less — a simplified affidavit, $1.00 filing fee, done in weeks (cannot transfer solely owned real estate; SCPA 1302)Minimal

Many assets skip court entirely: joint accounts, life insurance and retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, and assets in funded revocable trusts pass outside probate and don't count toward the $50,000 threshold.

Executor commissions (SCPA 2307): the statutory schedule

In New York the executor's pay is not negotiated — SCPA 2307 fixes it as a percentage of the property the executor receives and pays out, on a sliding scale like tax brackets. Each rate is split in half: one half for receiving the assets, one half for distributing them.

Five fine points that change the number:

  • It is separate from attorney fees. The executor gets paid and the lawyer gets paid. Family-member executors often waive some or all of the commission; nobody is required to work free, and commissions are taxable as ordinary income.
  • Specifically bequeathed property is excluded from the base (SCPA 2307(2)), as is real property the executor never sold that passes directly to the devisee.
  • Multiple fiduciaries multiply it. Under SCPA 2307(5), estates of $300,000 or more can support up to three full commissions when three or more fiduciaries serve — three co-executors on a $1M estate can mean over $100,000 in commissions.
  • Attorney-drafters face a haircut. Under SCPA 2307-a, an executor who is also the attorney that drafted the will gets only half the statutory commission unless the will contains a signed acknowledgment disclosing the dual role. This is a frequent planning trap.
  • The will controls first. If the will sets a compensation amount, that provision governs instead of the statutory schedule.

Attorney fees: no schedule, just "reasonable"

Unlike California and Florida, New York sets no statutory percentage for attorneys. New York probate attorneys charge hourly or flat fees that must be "reasonable" — and if anyone disputes a fee, the Surrogate's Court can fix it under SCPA 2110. Published practitioner ranges commonly run a few thousand dollars for simple estates to $10,000+ for complex ones, but there is no schedule to anchor a quote to, so families should get engagement letters in writing.

Filing fees (SCPA 2402(d))

The Surrogate's Court charges one mandatory fee to file the petition, based on the gross value of the probate estate (non-probate assets don't count). The schedule is uniform across all counties:

Under $10,000$45
$10,000 to under $20,000$75
$20,000 to under $50,000$215
$50,000 to under $100,000$280

The $1,250 cap means filing fees are the least painful line item for large estates — a $5M estate pays the same as a $500K one. Pay at filing by certified check, bank draft, or attorney escrow check; if later-discovered assets push the estate into a higher bracket, the difference comes due with the inventory or final accounting. Certified copies of letters run $6 per page — order 5–10 at the outset, since banks and title companies each want an original.

Voluntary administration: the $50,000 shortcut

SCPA Article 13 ("Voluntary Administration") is New York's small-estate shortcut: a simplified affidavit form, no formal accounting, a $1.00 filing fee, and certificates typically issued in 2–4 weeks. SCPA 1301 defines the qualifying estate precisely — and three details trip families up:

  1. $50,000 of personal property, gross. Debts don't reduce it: $55,000 in a checking account with $20,000 of credit card debt is over the limit. The threshold rose from $30,000 to $50,000 in 2009 and has not changed since.
  2. Personal property only — the real estate trap. Article 13 cannot transfer any interest in solely owned New York real property (SCPA 1302). A house, condo, or vacant lot in the decedent's sole name (or held as tenant in common) needs full probate or administration to change hands — the voluntary administrator can settle the personal property, but not the deed. Property held as joint tenants with right of survivorship or tenants by the entirety passes outside the estate and doesn't block the procedure.
  3. Certain property is excluded from the count. Exempt property set aside for a surviving spouse or minor children under EPTL 5-3.1 (e.g., one motor vehicle worth up to $25,000, household furnishings) doesn't count, nor do joint accounts, POD/TOD accounts, and other non-probate assets.

The procedure works for estates with or without a will, and for non-domiciliaries who left personal property in New York. But the voluntary administrator has limits: no power to pursue wrongful-death or personal-injury claims of the decedent (SCPA 1306).

Creditors: the 7-month window

New York creditors have seven months from the date letters are issued to present claims (SCPA 1802). An executor who distributes assets before that window closes can be held personally liable to a creditor who files a timely claim — one of the costliest traps for do-it-yourself executors. Publication of notice to creditors is not strictly required in New York, but practitioners recommend it to start the clock running and shorten the effective claim exposure. Funeral expenses, taxes, and administration expenses get paid first under the SCPA 1811 priority order.

Two New York specifics slow things down: every distributee must waive or be served — each heir signs a Waiver and Consent or is served with a citation — and where heirs are distant (cousins, nieces, nephews), the court demands kinship proof: family-tree affidavits, birth/death/marriage records, sometimes a full kinship hearing before letters issue. An improperly executed will (EPTL 3-2.1 requires the will be signed at the end and attested by two witnesses) or any valid objection turns the matter contested.

The estate tax cliff (2026: $7.35M)

New York runs its own estate tax, separate from the federal one — and for larger estates it can be the biggest "probate cost" of all. The 2026 numbers, set by the NYS Department of Taxation and Finance:

  • Basic exclusion: $7,350,000 for deaths in 2026 (up from $7,160,000 in 2025). Estates at or below it owe no New York estate tax.
  • The cliff: at 105% of the exclusion — $7,717,500 — the credit disappears entirely (Tax Law §952(c)). Above that line the graduated rate schedule (3.06%–16%, §952) applies to the whole estate from the first dollar, not just the excess. Practitioner tables put a $7,717,500 taxable estate at roughly $735,000 of tax — one extra dollar past the cliff line can cost hundreds of thousands.
  • No spousal portability. A surviving spouse cannot inherit the deceased spouse's unused New York exclusion — proper planning must use both spouses' exemptions while both are alive.
  • The return is due within 9 months of death (Form ET-706), with a completed federal Form 706 attached even when no federal return is required.

For context, the 2026 federal basic exclusion is $15,000,000 per person — far higher than New York's. An estate can easily owe New York tax while owing nothing federally. The three-year gift add-back (Tax Law §954(a)(3)) also pulls recent taxable gifts back into the New York gross estate.

Who inherits without a will

Under EPTL 4-1.1, when there is no will:

  • Surviving spouse + children: the spouse takes $50,000 plus half the remainder; the children split the other half.
  • Spouse only: the spouse takes everything.
  • Children only: the children take everything, equally.
  • No spouse or children: to parents, then siblings, then more distant relatives — the full table runs many tiers.

A spousal right of election (EPTL 5-1.1-A) also lets a surviving spouse claim at least one-third of the net estate even when the will says otherwise — one more reason wills must be drafted with the elective share in mind.

FAQ

How much does probate cost in New York?

Practitioner analyses commonly put total New York probate costs at 2–7% of the estate. The biggest line items are executor commissions fixed by statute (SCPA 2307) — $34,000 on a $1M estate — plus attorney fees (hourly or flat, no statutory schedule), a filing fee up to $1,250, appraisals, and potential New York estate tax.

How are executor commissions calculated in New York?

SCPA 2307 sets a sliding scale on property the executor receives and distributes: 5% on the first $100,000, 4% on the next $200,000, 3% on the next $700,000, 2.5% on the next $4M, and 2% over $5M — half on receiving, half on paying out. A $1M estate yields $34,000; a $500K estate yields $19,000.

What is the New York small estate limit?

$50,000 of solely owned personal property (SCPA Article 13, SCPA 1301). Voluntary administration uses a simplified affidavit form with a $1.00 filing fee and typically finishes in 2–4 weeks — but it cannot transfer solely owned real estate, which always requires full probate or administration.

How long does probate take in New York?

Expect 1–3 months to receive Letters Testamentary in smaller counties and 3–6 months in high-volume New York City Surrogates' Courts, with a full uncontested administration running roughly 9–18 months. Creditor claims can arrive for 7 months after letters issue, which sets a floor under the timeline.

What is the New York estate tax cliff?

For 2026 the New York basic exclusion is $7,350,000. Estates over 105% of that amount ($7,717,500) lose the credit entirely and the whole estate is taxed from the first dollar at rates of 3.06%–16%. New York's exclusion is not portable between spouses.
